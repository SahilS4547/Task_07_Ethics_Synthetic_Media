# Policy Limitations and Residual Risk

## Introduction

The Responsible Synthetic Media Policy reduces several predictable
risks, but it cannot guarantee that synthetic media will always be used
or interpreted responsibly.

Recognizing these limitations is important because a policy that
overstates its effectiveness may create false confidence.


## 1. Disclosure Can Be Removed

The policy requires disclosure, but disclosure is not permanent.

A third party can crop a video, remove a caption, rename a file, edit
an audio introduction, or repost only part of an artifact.

This limitation was visible in my Task 6 provenance work. The D-ID
artifact contained visible branding, but I recognized that a visible
watermark could potentially be cropped, covered, or removed.

The organization can control the original artifact but cannot fully
control every downstream copy.


## 2. Provenance Can Be Lost

Content credentials and metadata can improve traceability, but their
usefulness depends on preservation throughout the distribution chain.

Re-encoding, editing, screenshots, screen recording, and unsupported
platforms may weaken or remove provenance information.

I did not test these transformations during Task 6, so I cannot claim
that the provenance mechanisms associated with my artifacts would
survive them.


## 3. Detection Is Not Guaranteed

Detection technologies may provide useful signals but should not be
treated as perfect authenticity tests.

Task 6 did not include an automated deepfake detector. My evaluation
instead relied on visible provenance information and manual inspection.

Human inspection also has limitations. My D-ID video contained
repetitive movement and limited emotional variation, but a casual viewer
could still initially interpret the presenter as real.

Similarly, the ElevenLabs narration contained synthetic cues during
extended listening but could sound like ordinary professional narration
during a short clip.


## 4. Human Review Can Fail

This policy relies heavily on human judgment.

A reviewer may:

- Miss a factual problem.
- Misinterpret consent.
- Underestimate downstream risk.
- Fail to notice an inadequate disclosure.
- Approve an artifact that becomes misleading in another context.

Review reduces risk but cannot eliminate human error.


## 5. Consent Can Become Ambiguous

Documented consent does not resolve every future disagreement.

A person may approve one synthetic use but object to:

- Reuse in another campaign.
- Editing.
- Translation.
- A different audience.
- A different distribution platform.

Consent therefore needs boundaries, but even detailed documentation
cannot anticipate every future context.


## 6. Good-Faith Dependence

The policy works best when employees intend to follow it.

A well-intentioned employee may misunderstand a requirement or skip a
step under time pressure.

A malicious employee may intentionally bypass approval, remove
disclosure, or use unauthorized source material.

Policy can establish accountability and make misuse more difficult, but
a written document cannot physically prevent every intentional
violation.


## 7. External Actors Are Outside the Policy

The university can govern its employees and contractors but cannot
directly govern unrelated people who download, modify, or redistribute
its content.

An external actor could remove disclosures or combine legitimate
synthetic media with misleading information.

Incident-response procedures can reduce the resulting damage but cannot
guarantee immediate correction.


## 8. Scale Creates Oversight Problems

A review process that works for five artifacts may fail for five
thousand.

As synthetic-media production becomes increasingly automated,
organizations may be tempted to generate personalized content at a rate
that exceeds meaningful human review.

The policy does not completely solve this tension.


## 9. Technology Will Change

Synthetic-media technology is developing quickly.

Artifacts that currently contain noticeable visual or audio cues may
become increasingly difficult to distinguish from conventional
recordings.

Generation systems, provenance standards, and detection techniques may
also change.

A static policy will therefore become outdated.

Periodic review is essential.


## 10. Residual Risk

Even when every requirement is followed, some residual risk remains.

A properly disclosed and consensual synthetic artifact may still:

- Be misunderstood.
- Lose its disclosure.
- Be redistributed misleadingly.
- Be mistaken for authentic media.
- Be modified by another person.
- Produce an unexpected reputational effect.

The goal of governance should therefore not be to promise zero risk.

The more realistic goal is to identify foreseeable risks, reduce them,
document decisions, establish accountability, and respond effectively
when safeguards fail.


## Conclusion

This policy depends on multiple imperfect safeguards working together.

Disclosure informs audiences.

Consent protects identity.

Provenance provides information about origin.

Review catches foreseeable problems.

Incident response addresses failures.

None is sufficient alone.

The most important limitation of the policy is therefore also one of
its central lessons: responsible synthetic-media governance requires
continuous human judgment rather than blind confidence in either
technology or written rules.
